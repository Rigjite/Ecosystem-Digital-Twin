## §21 — Cryosphere and mountain hazards

## Sources and verification status

- ICIMOD, "Understanding the Gyirong–Rasuwa disaster of 26 August
  2026: A preliminary synthesis" (36-page report, published 13 October
  2026, lib.icimod.org record qdvc1-tc123) and ICIMOD press release of
  the same date, "Second major Himalayan flood in seven weeks raises
  alarm over compounding mountain hazards" (icimod.org) — **primary
  source for this section, fetched and read directly in this run (the
  full PDF was downloaded and text-extracted)**. Verified figures:
  ~132 million cubic metres of rock and glacier ice detached from the
  northern face of Langtang Lirung; release elevation ~5,200 masl,
  near-vertical fall ~1,200 m, total drop ~2,200 m, then ~450 m rise on
  the opposite slope; Sentinel-1 line-of-sight deformation up to 40 cm,
  7 July–24 August 2026 (48 days), NISAR ~30 cm over 24 days; source
  glacier ~29 m/yr during 2014–2024, fifth-fastest of 148 ice bodies,
  no acceleration trend — bedrock failure, ice passive; not a
  conventional GLOF; collapse 08:37 NST, surge reached the border
  corridor in ~7 minutes; 1,451 deaths and 5,745 missing in Nepal
  (NDRRMA, as of 21 September 2026), 43 deaths and 519 missing in
  China; 13 hydropower projects destroyed or damaged, ~one-tenth of
  Nepal's installed capacity; two temporary lakes formed downstream and
  breached on 28 and 31 August without secondary disaster; SMS alerts
  09:15–09:16, 679,295 messages sent; flood wave Galchhi 10:28,
  Malekhu 11:50, Mugling 13:00, Devghat 15:20; Trishuli rose up to 9 m
  within 30 minutes at Galchhi; Rasuwagadhi gauge stopped transmitting
  08:50; water-level sensors installed in the corridor "were not
  designed to detect an extremely rapid-onset rock–ice avalanche and
  debris-flow event"; possible contributors listed as geological
  weaknesses, damage from the 2015 earthquake, thawing permafrost,
  glacier change and snow/ice accumulation, no single trigger
  confirmed; seismic signal first reported as Ms 4.4, reassessed Ms 5.2
  (USGS) / Mw 5.7 (GFZ), consistent with mass movement; July 2025
  Purepu supraglacial-lake flood in the same corridor (which destroyed
  the Friendship Bridge); separate collapse confirmed in the Budhi
  Gandaki basin; deformation analysis was retrospective, and the report
  calls for the same techniques to be applied prospectively to screen
  analogous source zones.

- Reuters, 2 October 2026 (Gopal Sharma), "Nepal plans to drain four
  glacial lakes after deadly floods": design work and detailed studies
  for Thulagi, Lower Barun, Lumding Tsho and Hongu II (4,050–5,483 m)
  to begin in the fiscal year to mid-July 2027; ranked among Nepal's
  most potentially dangerous by a 2020 UNDP/ICIMOD assessment; ~$50
  million plan (Green Climate Fund, UNDP, government), up to six years;
  sensors, flood forecasting and early-warning systems. The "about 26
  GLOF events in four decades" figure is corroborated by the UNDP
  adaptation project page ("Since the 1970s, the country has
  experienced 26 GLOF events"; 47 glacial lakes currently classified
  as potentially dangerous).
- ICIMOD and UNDP (2020), *Inventory of glacial lakes and
  identification of potentially dangerous glacial lakes in the Koshi,
  Gandaki and Karnali river basins of Nepal, the Tibet Autonomous Region
  of China, and India* (UNDP Nepal press release): 47 potentially
  dangerous glacial lakes in total — 21 in Nepal, 25 in the Tibet
  Autonomous Region of China, 1 in India; 42 of the 47 in the Koshi
  basin, 3 in the Gandaki, 2 in the Karnali. The 47 is a three-basin,
  three-country total, not a Nepal-only count. The four lakes named for
  lowering (Thulagi, Lower Barun, Lumding Tsho, Hongu II) come from the
  2026 project (Reuters, above), not from the 2020 ranking.
- Copernicus SentiWiki, Sentinel-1 mission and applications pages:
  each Sentinel-1 satellite has a 12-day repeat cycle; the
  two-satellite constellation gives a 6-day repeat; Sentinel-1B was
  lost in December 2021 (12-day only until Sentinel-1C launched in
  December 2024 restored the 6-day cycle); Sentinel-1A operations
  ended 29 June 2026 and Sentinel-1D launched November 2025, returning
  the mission to a two-satellite (C/D) constellation; check the repeat
  interval for a given area and period against the actual acquisition
  plan.
- InSAR line-of-sight limits: conventional InSAR measures only one
  component of surface displacement, in the satellite's line of sight;
  slope movements perpendicular to the line of sight may go
  undetected; the technique is practically insensitive to north–south
  motion; foreshortening, layover and radar shadow cause data loss on
  steep slopes; temporal decorrelation from vegetation, snow and
  seasonal change (review: "Satellite-Based Differential Radar
  Interferometry in Landslide Research", Remote Sensing 2026, 18:1081;
  see also Remote Sensing 2022, 14:1759).
- Inverse-velocity / tertiary-creep failure forecasting: Fukuzono
  (1985) — the reciprocal of displacement velocity in the accelerated
  failure stage is approximately linearly related to the remaining time
  to failure; Saito (1965, 1969) for the creep-stage formulation.
  Established for soil and rock landslides; for rock-ice avalanches it
  is a research hypothesis, not a demonstrated precursor (see the
  Langtang note in the rate-and-acceleration row).
- Randolph Glacier Inventory (RGI 7.0, 2023; doi:10.5067/f6jmovy5navz)
  and GLIMS: RGI is a globally complete inventory of glacier outlines
  (a subset of the GLIMS multi-temporal database), a snapshot of the
  world's glaciers outside the ice sheets; it is not designed for
  glacier-by-glacier rates of area change, which need repeat mapping.
- Global Terrestrial Network for Permafrost (GTN-P, International
  Permafrost Association programme): database of borehole ground
  temperature time series (Thermal State of Permafrost, TSP) and
  active-layer thickness grids (Circumpolar Active Layer Monitoring,
  CALM), hosted at the Arctic Portal; measurement standards and
  guidelines at gtnp.arcticportal.org.
- NASA MEaSUREs ITS_LIVE global glacier velocity dataset, v2.0
  (The Cryosphere 19:3517–3533, 2025; doi:10.5194/tc-19-3517-2025):
  feature-tracked surface velocities from Landsat 4–9 and Sentinel-1/2,
  over 36 million image pairs since 1982, near-real-time processing.: feature-tracked
  surface velocities from Landsat 4–9 and Sentinel-1/2, over 36 million
  image pairs since 1982, near-real-time processing.

### Bozkurt flash flood, 11 August 2021 — sources

- Casualties, rescues, rainfall total: 82 died, 364 received medical support, 2,483 rescued from the flood waters; Bozkurt highest rainfall, 453 kg/m² — Disaster and Emergency Management Presidency (AFAD) 2022b, as cited in the peer-reviewed paper "Communication of flood early warnings and community response: the case of Sinop-Kastamonu, Türkiye", Natural Hazards (2025), DOI 10.1007/s11069-025-07286-0. Verified against the full text of the paper on 27 October 2026.
- Rainfall intensity and return period: 149.5 mm in three hours, peak 133.7 mm/h, exceeding a 500-year return period; rain from noon 10 August to midnight — Bozkurt–Ezine Stream Watershed case study, Kastamonu University, NbS4LD project (2026). Verified against the document itself on 27 October 2026 (Section 1, p. 2). Project case-study analysis, not a peer-reviewed paper — figures carried with that provenance.
- Death-toll discrepancy, noted honestly: the case study states 77 lives lost for the watershed flood, while AFAD 2022b (via Natural Hazards 2025) reports 82 deaths for the Western Black Sea event as a whole. The candidate keeps the AFAD event total (82) and records the case-study figure (77) with its scope; the two counts differ in scope and date, not by error.
- Evacuations: about 2,250 people evacuated across the region — AFAD, via Associated Press, 14 August 2021.
- Channel narrowing: the Ezine's bed and alluvial flood plain through Bozkurt narrowed by construction from about 400 m to about 15 m, with residential buildings along the waterfront — geologists, via Associated Press, 14 August 2021.
- No verified lead-time figure for the Bozkurt evacuation warning is recorded — a gap, not a zero. The Şanlıurfa flood of 2023 (16 died) is a separate event and is not used here.

- Roy P., Majumdar R., Chauhan P. et al. 26 August 2026 Rock–Ice
  Avalanche in Nepal: Rapid Assessment of the Cascading Disaster Using
  Satellite Data. J. Indian Soc. Remote Sens. (2026), published 16
  October 2026, DOI 10.1007/s12524-026-02611-x — independent rapid
  assessment, read via the publisher's page 2026-10-28: primary failure
  zone ~990,000 m², average scoop depth ~100 m, displaced volume
  ~99 (±10) million m³; NISAR L-band SBAS InSAR shows anomalously high
  velocity at the escarpment before failure, and NISAR pixel-offset

  movement in the same segment; Sentinel-2 glacier velocimetry and
  IceBoost/ITIBOV bed morphometry show an active, thick icefall there.

- Sah A. K. No detectable precursor in Sentinel-1 backscatter before
  the 26 August 2026 Langtang Lirung rock–ice avalanche, Nepal.

## §22 — Steppe and natural grassland

## Sources and verification status

- IUCN Temperate Grasslands Conservation Initiative (TGCI): temperate
  grasslands "the most altered and endangered ecosystem on the planet";
  about 8% of the Earth's terrestrial surface; only about 5% of that
  protected within the global protected-area system (TGCI Global Action
  Plan, IUCN 2010); the 2008 Hohhot workshop record (Peart 2008) gives
  the same ~8% figure with 5.5% protected. Converted for crops,
  plantations, urbanisation, energy and mining; much of the remainder
  under intensive grazing.
- IUCN World Conservation Congress Resolution WCC-2008-RES-44: temperate
  grasslands "one of the least protected types of biomes globally —
  barely 4.59% of their area is protected".
- IUCN World Conservation Congress 2025, Resolution WCC-2025-Res-005,
  "Protection and restoration of diverse native grasslands" (adopted
  from Motion 005; IUCN 2026, Resolutions, Recommendations and other
  Decisions, Abu Dhabi): ancient temperate grasslands have the lowest
  protection of Earth's 15 biomes, with less than half intact; urges
  expanded international cooperation to conserve mostly-intact temperate
  grassland, consistent with Target 3 of the Kunming-Montreal Global
  Biodiversity Framework. Note: the submitted motion's call to "secure
  protection of 30% of this endangered biome by 2030" does not appear in
  the adopted resolution, and the adopted text's list of grasslands does
  not include Australia's lowland grasslands (both were in the submitted
  motion draft).
- FAO, Global Assessment of Soil Carbon in Grasslands (Dondini et al.
  2023, FAO Animal Production and Health Paper No. 187, doi
  10.4060/cc3981en; first assessment; FAO LEAP Partnership funded):
  baseline SOC stocks in semi-natural and managed grasslands; grasslands
  hold about 20% of the world's SOC; a 0.3% SOC increase in the 0–30 cm
  layer over 20 years of improved management could sequester 0.3 t
  C/ha/yr; negative carbon balance found in East Asia, Central and South
  America and Africa south of the Equator.
- UNCCD, Scientific Conceptual Framework for Land Degradation Neutrality
  (Science-Policy Interface; Orr et al. 2017, UNCCD SPI report, Bonn;
  journal version Cowie et al. 2018, Environ. Sci. Policy 79:25–35,
  doi 10.1016/j.envsci.2017.10.011): the three LDN indicators are land cover
  (land-cover change), land productivity (net primary production) and
  carbon stocks (soil organic carbon); neutrality is managed at
  landscape scale per land type; response hierarchy avoid → reduce →
  reverse.
- UNCCD Good Practice Guidance for SDG indicator 15.3.1 (Sims et al.
  2021, Version 2.0, UNCCD, Bonn): the three sub-indicators above;
  one-out-all-out (1OAO) aggregation — an area is degraded if any
  sub-indicator shows degradation — with guidance on identifying false
  positives/negatives where 1OAO gives a counterintuitive verdict.
- Sims et al., "A land degradation interpretation matrix for reporting on
  UN SDG indicator 15.3.1 and land degradation neutrality" (Environ.
  Sci. Policy, 2020, doi 10.1016/j.envsci.2020.07.015; UNCCD
  Library): 1OAO can give counterintuitive verdicts — invasive-plant
  removal lowering biomass, woody encroachment, land remediation — and
  countries may report false positives/negatives with justification.
- Grazing utilisation rule of thumb: "take half, leave half" — a 50%
  proper-use factor on annual growth — the rangeland profession's
  long-standing rule of thumb (Univ. of Arizona Extension, "Limitations
  to Estimating Production on Rangelands"; Univ. of Wyoming Extension
  B-1320, "Animal Unit Month Concepts and Applications for Grazing
  Rangelands"), with the documented caveats that it is commonly
  misunderstood and that only ~25% of production is actually available
  to livestock once trampling, wildlife and waste are counted; lower
  rates in arid conditions.
- FAO Desert Locust Bulletin glossary (FAO Locust Watch): density
  classes for non-gregarious adults and hoppers — isolated <25/ha (0–1
  adult per 400 m foot transect), scattered 25–500/ha (1–20 adults),
  group ≥500/ha (20+ adults) — defined for Desert Locust
  (*Schistocerca gregaria*).
- Marmot density case values (not standards): tarbagan 2.0–3.0 habitable
  holes/ha in the Dzeren valley before the mid-20th century (current
  status of *Marmota sibirica* in Southeast Transbaikalia); bobak family
  density 0.4/ha on favourable habitat, 0.1/ha maximum on marginal
  habitat (Tokarski et al. 1991, via the marmot literature review).
  No standardised density threshold was found for either species.
- Pellant et al., Interpreting Indicators of Rangeland Health, USDI
  Bureau of Land Management TR 1734-6 (2000; version 5, 2020: Pellant,
  Shaver, Pyke, Herrick, Lepak, Riegel, Kachergis, Newingham, Toledo &
  Busby): 17 indicators rated to assess three attributes — soil/site
  stability, hydrologic function, biotic integrity — for a moment-in-time
  assessment, explicitly not for temporal monitoring. Indicators: rills,
  water-flow patterns, pedestals/terracettes, bare ground, gullies, wind
  scour/depositional areas, litter movement, soil resistance to erosion,
  soil surface loss/degradation, plant composition relative to
  infiltration/runoff, compaction layer, functional/structural groups,
  plant mortality/decadence, litter amount, annual production, invasive
  plants, reproductive capability of perennials.
- Tongway & Hindley (2004), Landscape Function Analysis (LFA): 11 soil
  surface features (soil cover, litter cover, canopy cover, crust
  brokenness, cryptogam cover, erosion degree/type, accumulated
  materials, surface roughness, surface resistance to erosion, texture,
  slake test) computed into three indices — soil stability, water
  infiltration capacity, nutrient cycling potential.
- Valkó et al. 2018, Science of the Total Environment 622–623:783–789:
  European forest-steppe regions characterised by low-frequency,
  cool-season, low-severity fires with an average return interval of
  several decades (citing Archibald et al. 2013; Lysenko 2008);
  characteristic species cannot tolerate very short fire return periods;
  regular spring burning regulates biomass but does not maintain
  biodiversity.
- Whisenant 1990, in Proceedings — Ecology and Management of Annual
  Rangelands (USDA Forest Service INT-GTR-313, pp. 170–175):
  pre-settlement fire-return intervals in sagebrush steppe probably
  60–110 years; much of the region now burns at intervals under 5 years
  after cheatgrass (*Bromus tectorum*) invasion creates a continuous
  fuelbed. Caveat from the USDA FEIS synthesis: Whisenant did not
  document how these estimates were derived — treat as a case value,
  not a measured standard.
- UNESCO World Heritage, "Landscapes of Dauria" (whc.unesco.org,
  document 159699; inscribed 2017, criteria ix and x): transboundary
  serial property, 912,624 ha; Daurian steppe ecosystem over 1 million
  km²; threatened birds (white-naped crane, hooded crane, swan goose,
  relict gull, great bustard, saker falcon) and endangered tarbagan
  marmot; up to 3 million birds in spring and 6 million in autumn on the
  East Asian–Australasian Flyway; critical summer grounds and autumn
  migration routes of the Mongolian gazelle.
- Daursky Nature Reserve (Wikipedia, corroborated by UNESCO listing):
  established 1987; 222,965 ha in southern Zabaykalsky Krai; core area
  about 49,764 ha in 9 plots; buffer zone about 173,201 ha including the
  Torey Lakes (Barun-Torey and Zun-Torey); Daurian forest-steppe
  ecoregion; subarctic climate, dry winter (Köppen Dwc).
- Great bustard as indicator: great bustard presence essentially
  determined by the extent of steppic/pseudosteppic habitats (Mata et
  al. 2025, Sci. Rep. 15:32617, doi 10.1038/s41598-025-12012-8, on
  high-speed-railway bird mortality); IUCN Vulnerable; steppe eagle
  *Aquila nipalensis* Endangered; great bustard not a good surrogate
  for all agro-steppe species (Perlman 2019, doctoral thesis,
  University of East Anglia — Extremadura/Alentejo subpopulations
  monitored 1985–2015, spring 2017 SPA field counts; further indicator
  species recommended).

## §32 — Non-peat wetlands and marshes

## Sources and verification status

- **Ramsar Convention on Wetlands, Global Wetland Outlook 2018** (first
  Global Wetland Outlook) — inland and coastal wetlands cover more than
  12.1 M km², 54% permanently and 46% seasonally inundated; about 35% of
  natural wetlands lost between 1970 and 2015, three times the rate of
  forest loss, with the loss rate accelerating after 2000; human-made
  wetlands (largely rice paddy and reservoirs) almost doubled over the
  period and now form 12% of wetlands. Figures read from the Ramsar GWO
  summary (clmeplus.org reprint of the Ramsar summary PDF).
- **Ramsar Convention, Article 1.1 (1971)** — the wetland definition
  quoted in the extent row.
- **Davidson, N. C. 2014.** How much wetland has the world lost?
  Long-term and recent trends in global wetland area. *Marine and
  Freshwater Research* 65(10):934. DOI 10.1071/MF14173. Long-term loss of
  natural wetlands averages 54–57%, as high as 87% since 1700; 64–71%
  lost since 1900; loss 3.7 times faster in the 20th and early 21st
  centuries than before.
- **Fluet-Chouinard, E. et al. 2023.** Extensive global wetland loss
  over the past three centuries. *Nature* 614:281–286. DOI
  10.1038/s41586-022-05572-6. About 3.4 million km² of inland wetlands
  lost since 1700 (CI 2.9–3.8), a net loss of about 21% (CI 16–23%),
  mainly to cropland — lower than earlier estimates extrapolated from
  high-loss regions. The catalogue presents Davidson (2014) and
  Fluet-Chouinard et al. (2023) together as a range of estimates.
- **Davis, S. M. & Ogden, J. C. (eds.) 1994.** The Everglades: The
  Ecosystem and Its Restoration. St. Lucie Press. Historic extent
  ~4,000 sq mi; just over half remains, compartmentalised by levees and
  canals; hydroperiod classes (sloughs 11+ months, sawgrass ridges 9–10
  months, short-hydroperiod marshes 3–7 months); slope ~2.5 inches per
  mile. Values read from the Everglades overview summary of this volume
  (ResearchGate publication 334069267) — the volume is the source of the
  landscape classes, the overview the access route. Where the overview
  and the Florida Museum differ (remaining extent "just over half" vs
  "less than half"; slope ~2.5 vs "less than two inches per mile, 3 cm

  mile or less" and names both sources (T98, 2026-10-28).
- **Florida Museum (University of Florida), South Florida Aquatic
  Environments: Florida Everglades** — once 4,000 sq mi (10,360 km²),
  now less than half that size; slope "less than two inches per mile
  (3 cm per km)"; sheet flow ~100 miles (161 km) to Florida Bay (page
  read 2026-10-28).
- **Sklar, F. H. et al. 2005.** The ecological–societal underpinnings of
  Everglades restoration. *Frontiers in Ecology and the Environment*
